01The Exam Question — Formalizing the Crawler's Dilemma

Why formalize at all

Picture a hall with n slot machines (the name "bandit" comes from the one-armed kind). Machine i pays out with unknown probability μᵢ. You have exactly T rounds. Each round you pull one machine and observe one reward. How do you spend your rounds?

This is not an analogy we reached for — it is a reduction. In LOGY, the machines are identities, the payout probability is each identity's true success rate against the target, the rounds are requests, and "one reward per pull" is exactly the cruel constraint of crawling: you only learn about the identity you used. Pull identity #3, and you learn nothing about #4, #5, #6. That one-sided feedback is the defining difficulty of the whole field.

EQ. 1.1 — The formal statement
machines: i ∈ {1..n}, unknown payout probabilities μ₁..μₙ horizon: T rounds total goal: maximize Σ rewards — equivalently, minimize regret

Why formalize: without a formal statement you cannot measure an answer. With one, you can say "this strategy is provably better" instead of "this one feels smarter" — and poster № 05's whole ledger design becomes testable mathematics instead of folklore.

02The Wrong Answer — A Smart Student's Proposal, Dissected

Why being wrong here is instructive, not embarrassing

Given the problem, a smart student answered: "Try every machine — say 5 iterations each — then compute the entropy of each one." Grade the pieces:

  • "Try every machine first" — correct family. This is genuinely a known algorithm class: Explore-Then-Commit (ETC). Uniform exploration, then commit. Respectable instinct.
  • "5 iterations" — statistically empty. To tell a μ=0.5 machine from a μ=0.6 machine with 95% confidence, Chernoff–Hoeffding says you need n ≥ ln(2/δ)/2Δ² ≈ 370 samples. With 5, one lucky win moves your estimate by 20%. You are not measuring — you are narrating noise.
  • "Compute the entropy" — the wrong statistic entirely. Entropy measures the spread of a distribution, not its quality. Watch it fail:
The counterexample that kills entropy ranking
MachineBehaviorOutcome entropyEntropy ranking
Apays every single pull0 bits (constant!) last place
Bfair coin — 50/50 win/lose1 bit (maximum)first place

Entropy peaks at the coin flip. Rank by it and you reward mediocrity and punish the guaranteed winner — the exact inversion of the goal. The correct statistic is the empirical mean x̄ᵢ = wins / pulls. (Entropy has a legitimate role in this system — measuring the human-likeness of timing patterns, poster № 05 § 08 — just not here.)

The third, deeper miss: ETC commits once. But the WAF is an adversary that changes its mind — the identity that was clean at page 50 may be burned at page 200. A one-shot decision is frozen into a world that moves. (§ 06 handles this.)

03The Real Difficulty — Why This Problem Is Hard When Its Code Is Trivial

Computational complexity vs statistical complexity

Here is the twist that puts bandits above intermediate material: the algorithm is trivial to run and impossible to shortcut. Each step costs O(n) arithmetic. There is no NP-hardness here. The difficulty lives somewhere else entirely — in sample complexity: how many observations does any strategy need before its decisions are right?

The question is not "can we solve it?" — it's "how many pulls does the truth cost?"

This is the dividing line between classical complexity theory and learning theory. In the bandit world the yardstick is regret — the price of not knowing:

EQ. 3.1 — Regret: the formal measure of every mistake
R(T) = T · μ* − E[ Σₜ μ_{A(t)} ] # μ* = best machine's true rate # "what we'd have earned pulling the best machine every time, # minus what we actually earned" a learner = a strategy whose R(T)/T → 0

Both stupid strategies have linear regret: pure exploitation locks onto the wrong machine forever (R grows linearly), pure exploration wastes a fixed fraction of every round (also linear). A learner must be sublinear — and how sublinear is exactly what the next section buys.

04UCB1 — Optimism in the Face of Uncertainty

The algorithm, then the reason for every symbol

Auer, Cesa-Bianchi & Fischer (2002). The full algorithm, production form:

EQ. 4.1 — UCB1, complete
# init: play every machine once for i in 1..n: play(i); record x̄ᵢ, nᵢ = 1 # every round t afterwards: for every machine i: score(i) = x̄ᵢ + √( 2 ln t / nᵢ ) play(argmax score); update x̄ᵢ, nᵢ

The philosophy, stated once and taken seriously: be optimistic about the uncertain. A machine you know little about gets credited with a bonus — treat its unknown as potential, sample it, and let evidence settle the argument. The bonus is not a heuristic; it is a confidence interval, computed so that the true μᵢ stays inside the interval with overwhelming probability. "Play the optimistic estimate" is "play the machine whose best case is highest" — and best cases converge to truth as evidence piles up.

Why the two terms behave the way they must:

  • x̄ᵢ — exploitation. What you've seen. It pulls selection toward proven quality.
  • √(2 ln t / nᵢ) — exploration with a decay schedule. Three properties fall out of the algebra automatically: grows when nᵢ is small (untested machines get their chance), shrinks as nᵢ grows (proven machines speak from their record), and grows only logarithmically in t (late in the run, even a totally untested machine gets one chance soon, not endless chances).
Worked example — the bonus rescuing an uncertain better arm

Twenty rounds later, b's bonus has shrunk and the pool settles wherever the truth is. Note what did not happen: no threshold was tuned, no schedule was set. The decay is inside the formula.

05The Guarantee — Why This Is the Shortest Possible Road

What is actually proven, and why it matters

The proof skeleton (the honest three-step version):

  • Step 1 — concentration. Chernoff–Hoeffding: the empirical mean deviates from the true mean by more than ε with probability ≤ 2e^(−2nε²). With the bonus set to √(2 ln t / nᵢ), the optimistic estimate stays above the true μᵢ "almost always" — and the "almost" tightens exponentially with every pull.
  • Step 2 — decomposition. Regret splits over machines: each bad machine's total contribution equals (its suboptimality Δᵢ) × (number of times it was pulled). So bounding regret = bounding pulls on bad machines.
  • Step 3 — the punchline. The bonus shrinks as ln t, and each bad pull raises the good machines' bonus relative to it — so bad machines self-limit. Result:
EQ. 5.1 — The UCB1 regret bound (Auer et al. 2002)
R(T) ≤ O( Σᵢ ln T / Δᵢ ) # Δᵢ = μ* − μᵢ

And the other half, which is the beautiful part: Lai–Robbins (1985) proved a lower bound — no algorithm can do better than Ω(ln T / Δᵢ) for every problem instance. Two independent results, twenty years apart, meeting exactly:

Upper bound = lower bound. UCB1 is optimal up to constants. There is no smarter road — only faster constants.

Why sublinear regret means "learning": R(T) growing like ln T means average regret R(T)/T → 0. Double your crawl and your rate of wasted requests doesn't double — it falls. The system's mistakes become history, not habit.

06Thompson Sampling — The Bayesian Sibling

Same order of optimality, different philosophy, zero hyperparameters

Thompson (1933 — rejected by reviewers, forgotten for 70 years, then resurrected by Google's ad systems). The alternative solution:

EQ. 6.1 — Thompson sampling, complete
for every round: for every machine i: draw θᵢ ~ Beta(αᵢ, βᵢ) # sample from the belief, not the mean play(argmax θᵢ) αᵢ += reward; βᵢ += (1 − reward)

Why exploration happens without being written down: an uncertain machine has a wide posterior — its samples sometimes land high, so it gets played, and sometimes low, so it waits. As data accumulates the posterior narrows, sampling converges to the mean, and exploration dies at exactly the rate the evidence justifies. No `t`, no bonus formula, no schedule — the uncertainty itself schedules the exploration.

And this is literally poster № 05's ledger: the IdentityLedger maintains Beta(α,β) per identity, and the "sticky"/"weighted" selection modes sample from (or near) those posteriors. Thompson is the fully-sampled version of what we already built. The UCB1 mode added in this release is its frequentist sibling — same optimal order, different metaphysics, and (below) a different failure mode against the adversary.

07The Adversary Breaks the Model — And What We Do About It

The honest asterisk on every proof above

Both UCB1 and Thompson assume rewards are drawn from fixed distributions. The WAF is not fixed — it is a player: it escalates against repetition, cools down after quiet, and reacts to your strategy. Under a changing μᵢ, the ln T regret bound does not just weaken — it can silently break, because the "best machine" the analysis optimizes toward stops existing.

The formal answer: adversarial bandits — the nonstochastic model (Auer et al. 2002b), solved by EXP3: exponential weights over estimated rewards, with importance weighting to handle the one-sided feedback:

EQ. 7.1 — EXP3's update (weights, not posteriors)
P(t+1, i) ∝ exp( η · ĝᵢ(t) ) # ĝ = reward estimate scaled by 1/P(played) R(T) = O( √(n T ln n) ) # sublinear even vs an adaptive adversary

The engineering answer (what LOGY ships): rather than full EXP3, we armor the stochastic learner against non-stationarity with three mechanisms from poster № 05:

  • Cooldowns — a benched identity returns to eligibility after 300s, so a temporarily-burned arm can recover when the WAF's mood cools. Non-stationarity is survived by forgetting slowly, not by discarding evidence.
  • The WAF state filter — the log-odds observer is, in bandit language, a change detector: it estimates when the environment shifted, which is precisely the assumption UCB1's proof requires you to notice.
  • Sticky's 30% churn — a permanent, small exploration floor that keeps sampling every arm, so a recovery is observed, not assumed.

The composed claim, precisely: UCB1 gives the optimal steady-state policy; the armor gives recovery when steady state is violated. Neither alone is enough; together they match what EXP3 guarantees, with better constants in the common (mildly adversarial) case.

09Where It Lives — The 40 Lines in Production

Because a proof that never ships is a paper

The selection is now a three-way policy in brain.py, chosen per job from the UI:

brain.py — the shipped implementation (abridged to its decision core)
def _pick_ucb1(self, eligible): plays = {k: identity(k).successes + identity(k).blocks for k in eligible} untried = [k for k in eligible if plays[k] == 0] if untried: return random.choice(untried) # init phase: measure the unknown total = sum(plays.values()) def score(k): s = identity(k) return s.successes/(s.successes+s.blocks) + math.sqrt(2*math.log(total)/plays[k]) return max(eligible, key=score)

Engineering notes on the mapping: nᵢ is not a separate counter — it is α+β, the posterior concentration from № 05, which means UCB1 and Thompson read the same ledger. The untried-first branch is UCB1's initialization played exactly as specified. And cooldown filtering sits outside the score: a benched arm isn't scored at all — optimism must never select an identity the breaker just parked.

Which mode, when: sticky for human-shape under stable targets; weighted when you want pure posterior behavior with zero stickiness; ucb1 for fresh pools against new targets — it is the fastest legitimate way to map a target's tolerance, because it front-loads measurement exactly where the uncertainty is.

10Honest Limits — Where the Optimism Stops Paying

Failure modes, priced
  • Stationarity is an assumption, not a fact. Every guarantee above conditions on fixed μᵢ. Against a responsive WAF the bound is not "weaker" — it is unproven. That is why § 07's armor exists and why the default mode is sticky, not UCB1.
  • Optimism has a cost during exploration. UCB1 will play bad arms periodically, forever (that's the ln term). Against a target where a single block is catastrophic (burns the IP for the whole job), that price can exceed the information bought. Weighted/sticky sampling loses the optimality proof but never over-invests in a dying arm.
  • The mean is not the whole story. UCB1 ranks by success rate; it is blind to latency. A 99%-clean identity that takes 9s per page may be worse for throughput than a 95% one at 0.4s. Fixing this properly means reward shaping (e.g. reward = success/latency) — a real extension, deliberately not smuggled in.
  • One-sided feedback is the real tax. You only observe the arm you played. All the ln T cost of bandits is, at bottom, the price of that blindness. Full-information problems (you see every arm's reward) admit much faster learners — the crawler's constraint is what makes the problem interesting.
